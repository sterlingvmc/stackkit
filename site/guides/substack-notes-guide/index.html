Substack Notes guide: how to post, schedule and measure Notes (2026)

Updated 2026-11-12 · Every setting below checked against Substack's help center and announcements · Independent, not affiliated with Substack Inc.

The short version: Notes are Substack's public short-form feed. They don't land in anyone's inbox, so they work best as conversation: reply to and restack other writers, post a few Notes you'd be glad to be found by, and point people to your posts. Substack's own scheduler (free, built in since spring 2026) handles one Note at a time; you only need a third-party tool if you want to batch a week at once or see which Notes actually bring subscribers.

What Notes are, and who sees them

Substack describes Notes as a place where creators publish short-form posts and share ideas with each other and their readers: posts, links, images, quotes and comments, shared with your subscribers and the wider Substack network. Two facts shape how you should use them:

  • Notes are not emailed. According to Substack's help center, subscribers aren't emailed when you post a Note, the way they are for a newsletter. They may see Notes they missed in a Notes roundup digest or a push notification.
  • Notes are public. They can appear on the web, in the Substack app and on your profile. Anyone can read them, but only signed-in Substack users can post, like or reply. Your subscribers see your Notes, restacks and engagement in their Home feed.

So treat a Note as a short public post that strangers might find, not as a message to your list. Your newsletter is still where you talk to subscribers directly.

1. Post a Note (web and app)

  • On the web: click Create, choose Note, write it, and post. The web composer lets you add multiple images or a video and choose who can reply.
  • In the app: tap the plus sign, write, and select Post.
  • Drafts: on the web, enter some text and click Cancel to save a draft; reopen drafts from the Drafts button in the composer. In the app, tap Cancel and choose to save to drafts.
  • Paid-only replies: if you have payments turned on, the web composer lets you limit replies to Only paid subscribers. Useful for a members-only discussion; for growth, leave replies open.

2. Schedule Notes with Substack's free built-in scheduler

Substack added Notes scheduling in spring 2026, on web, iOS and Android. It's free and needs no extension:

  • Web: in the Notes composer, click the calendar icon, pick a date and time, click Save, then click Schedule.
  • App: in the composer, tap the three dots at the top, choose Schedule, pick the time, tap Save, then Schedule.
  • Times are in your local time zone, and scheduled Notes wait in your Drafts, where you can keep editing until they publish.

The limit is that it's one Note at a time. Writer Carrie (Thrive with Carrie) also reports that you can queue Notes up to three months ahead and unschedule one by clicking the X next to its schedule. If scheduling five Notes on a Sunday feels fine, you don't need anything else.

3. When a third-party scheduler is worth it

Batch tools earn their price when you write many Notes in one sitting. The cheapest serious option we've found is SubflowAI; NotesIQ is a one-time $4.99; NoteStack and WriteStack combine scheduling with analytics; StackSweller is a plain cloud queue. Before you pay, check how each one publishes: some hand Notes to Substack's own scheduler, and others keep a queue on their own servers that holds your Substack session. Either way, check that Notes publish with your laptop closed. We compare all of them, including Substack's free scheduler, on the Substack Notes schedulers page.

Almost all of these tools work through your logged-in Substack session or Substack's unofficial API. Prefer tools that let you approve every Note, and avoid anything that follows, likes or replies to people automatically.

4. Spend more time replying than posting

Scheduling makes it easy to post a lot, which isn't the same as growing. When Notes scheduling launched, writing coach Sarah Fay (Substack Writers at Work) warned against randomly scheduling lots of Notes or posting three to five times a day, and said the algorithm rewards you for engaging with others more than for getting engagement yourself. A sustainable routine:

  • Reply thoughtfully to a few writers in your niche each day, the way you'd comment at a dinner party, not with "Great post!"
  • Restack posts you genuinely recommend, with a line on why.
  • Answer every reply on your own Notes; that's where readers decide whether to subscribe.
  • When someone keeps showing up, remember them. Orbit (paid) or the free, self-hosted Circles keep a record of who you've talked to; a spreadsheet works too.

5. Know what to post

Notes reward things a stranger can get value from without clicking. Formats that fit the feed:

  • A single useful idea from your latest post, stated in full, with the link as an optional extra.
  • A question your readers can answer from experience.
  • A quick win: one small step someone can do in five minutes.
  • A list you made. Since October 29, 2026, Substack lists (themed collections of writers you recommend) can be shared on Notes, where they appear as a card in the feed. Our recommendations and lists guide covers how to make one.

Write the Note in your own voice. AI tools can help you pull ideas out of an old post, but edit everything before it goes out.

6. Measure which Notes bring subscribers

Likes and restacks don't tell you whether a Note grew your list. A few tools try to connect Notes to new subscribers:

  • Subshack (free) ranks which Notes and posts brought subscribers and audits your public profile.
  • Substack Insights (free under 1,000 subscribers) and PlotStack (free) put your Notes and post numbers in one place.
  • NoteStack, NotesIQ and WriteStack add best-time heatmaps next to their schedulers.

Check your numbers monthly, not daily. Look for the two or three Note formats that bring subscribers, then write more of those.

A 20-minute daily Notes routine

  1. Reply to three Notes or posts from writers in your niche (10 minutes).
  2. Answer replies on your own Notes (5 minutes).
  3. Post or schedule one Note: an idea, a question or a quick win (5 minutes).
  4. Once a month, check which Notes brought subscribers and adjust.

Tools mentioned in this guide

  • SubflowAI: Batch-scheduling a week or month of Notes cheaply, with a visual calendar and AI Note drafts from your articles. Pro Monthly $9.97/mo; Pro 5-Year $69.97 one-time; Premium $99.97 (5 years, includes Connect to Claude/ChatGPT). Connect is a $30 one-time add-on on Pro plans. 5-day free trial, no card; 14-day money-back guarantee.
  • WriteStack: Writers who live on Substack Notes and want batch scheduling, research and Notes-to-subscriber analytics. Hobbyist $23.99/mo or $19.99/mo billed yearly ($239.88/yr); Standard $32.99/mo or $24.99/mo billed yearly ($299.88/yr); Enterprise $99.99/mo or $79.99/mo billed yearly ($959.88/yr). Every plan has a 7-day free trial.
  • NoteStack: Notes-first writers who want subscriber-conversion rankings, best-time heatmaps and a scheduler in one extension. Starter $8/mo or $60/yr (capped scheduler: 7 new Notes/mo); Pro $12/mo or $99/yr (no caps, adds AI Ghostwriter and Post Coach). 7-day free trial.
  • NotesIQ: Writers who want a cheap, one-time-payment Notes dashboard with a best-time heatmap and a weekly queue. $4.99 one-time for lifetime access (analytics, scheduler, competitor lookups); 7-day money-back guarantee.
  • StackSweller: A simple cloud queue for batch-scheduling Substack Notes. Pro $39/mo, or $25/mo billed yearly ($250/yr). 7-day free trial.
  • Subshack: Finding out which Notes and posts actually bring subscribers, and auditing your profile. Free (Subshack 1.0), including a no-signup public profile audit.
  • Substack Insights: Writers who want a cheap, private look at their best posts, best publishing times and Notes engagement. Free for publications under 1,000 subscribers; $9.99 one-time above that. The pricing card lists Notes analytics and conversion stats under the paid tier.
  • PlotStack: Writers who want Substack's stats, growth, posts and Notes numbers in one local dashboard, with CSV and PNG exports. Free. No paid tier or in-app purchases listed; its maker says his newsletter tools are free and will stay free.
  • Orbit: Writers growing through relationships who want to remember who they've talked to across Notes, comments and DMs. $12/mo or $120/yr, included with a paid subscription to creator Rebecca Spitzer's Substack (What's Working Right Now on Substack). No free plan or trial.
  • Circles: Technical writers who want a private, self-hosted CRM of everyone they talk to on Substack, with reply queues, timelines and an MCP server for Claude. Free and open source (MIT license). You host it yourself; any server or database costs are yours. The Circles Companion Chrome extension is free on the Chrome Web Store.
