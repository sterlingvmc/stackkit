Substack images guide: sizes, thumbnails, free photos and alt text (2026)

Updated 2026-11-20 · Sizes and steps checked against Substack's help center; photo licenses checked on Unsplash and Pexels · Independent, not affiliated with Substack Inc.

The short version: make post preview images at least 1200 x 630 px and keep the subject near the center, because Substack crops thumbnails to different shapes in different places (you can fix the crop on the web). Get free photos from Unsplash or Pexels, make headers in Canva, shrink files with Squoosh before you upload, and add alt text to every image that carries meaning.

1. The sizes Substack recommends

From Substack's help center article on image dimensions (updated November 12, 2026, when the new website editor launched):

ImageRecommended sizeWhere you add it
Social / post preview image1200 x 630 px or largerPost settings
Full-width post imageUp to 1:1; taller images are cropped to 1:1Post editor
Logo256 x 256 px or larger, transparent backgroundWebsite editor → Assets → Logo
Wordmark1344 x 256 px or larger (wide, horizontal)Website editor → Assets → Wordmark
Header cover photo1600 px wide or widerWebsite editor → Header card → Layout: Cover (Classic theme only)
Welcome image600 x 600 px or largerWebsite editor → Welcome → Intro
Email banner1100 x 220 px PNG, transparent backgroundSettings → Email header

The same article lists the aspect ratios Substack uses: post thumbnails are 3:2, and its portrait, square and landscape shapes are 9:16, 1:1 and 16:9. A 1200 x 630 image is close to the 1.91:1 that LinkedIn uses, so one preview image usually works for sharing too.

2. Thumbnails: why faces get cut off, and how to fix it

Your post's social preview image is also its thumbnail across Substack, and Substack shows it in three shapes:

  • Landscape: posts shared in Notes, dashboard thumbnails on desktop, and several home page layouts.
  • Square: "Top Posts" and other home page layouts, and the Posts tab in the Substack app.
  • Portrait: image assets in your Share Center.

If you don't adjust anything, Substack uses a smart crop that picks the area with the most detail or contrast, which is how heads and titles get chopped. To set it yourself on the web: click Continue when you're ready to publish, scroll to Social preview, click Edit, then Adjust. Drag the crop box corners, move the focal point, and check the Portrait, Square and Landscape previews. For posts already published, go to your Posts page and click Adjust on the thumbnail. Cropping is web-only for now.

Practical rule: put the face or the text you care about in the middle third of the image, and leave plain background at the edges.

3. Free photos you're allowed to use

  • Unsplash: all images can be downloaded and used for free, including commercially, with no permission needed. Attribution is appreciated but not required. You can't sell images without significant modification. Unsplash+ images are a separate paid library with their own license.
  • Pexels: all photos and videos are free to use, credit is appreciated but not required, and its license names newsletters as an allowed use. Don't show identifiable people in a bad or offensive light, don't imply that people or brands in a photo endorse you, and don't use a photo in your logo or business name.

Popular free photos appear on thousands of sites. Cropping tightly, adding your title, or using a consistent color treatment makes them look like yours. A short credit line in the caption ("Photo by … on Unsplash") is a nice habit even though neither site requires it.

4. Making your own headers and graphics

  • Canva is the default for header, preview and Notes images, and the free plan covers most writers. Set a custom 1200 x 630 canvas once and save it as a template.
  • Kittl is stronger on typography-heavy, stylized graphics; its site is currently inconsistent about commercial use on the free plan, so read its license before using it for a paid or business newsletter. Side by side: Canva vs Kittl for Substack images.
  • Figma if you want a precise, reusable visual system and don't mind a steeper learning curve.
  • Coolors to pick a palette once and reuse the same hex codes in your images and in Substack's website editor.
  • remove.bg for cutting out a headshot or product photo; its free preview-size download is often enough for a small inline image.
  • Excalidraw for quick hand-drawn diagrams, and Datawrapper for charts (Substack has a help article on embedding Datawrapper charts).

Since November 12, 2026, Substack's own website editor handles your site's themes, colors, fonts, header and logo, so you only need outside tools for the images themselves. See the note on the Design tools page.

5. Shrink images before you upload

Exports from design tools and phone screenshots are often several megabytes. Run them through Squoosh first: drop an image in, compare before and after with a slider, pick a format and quality, and save. It's free, runs in your browser so the image isn't uploaded to a server, and handles one image at a time. Smaller files load faster for readers on phones.

6. Alt text and captions

Alt text is a written description of an image for readers using screen readers, and for anyone whose images don't load. In the Substack post editor, click the three dots in the upper-right corner of an image and choose Edit alt text, type a clear, concise description, and select Ok. Edit caption in the same menu adds a visible caption under the image, which is the natural place for a photo credit.

The same menu (on the web editor) also has Set full width, plus Crop, Filter, Finetune (brightness, contrast and more), Annotate and Redact (blur part of an image), so small fixes such as blurring an email address in a screenshot don't need another app.

Write alt text that says what the image shows and why it's there: "Chart: paid subscribers grew from 40 to 120 between June and October" helps more than "chart".

7. A simple routine for every post

  1. Pick or make one 1200 x 630 image with the subject centered.
  2. Compress it with Squoosh.
  3. Upload it, add alt text and (for stock photos) a credit caption.
  4. Before publishing, open Social preview → Edit → Adjust and check all three thumbnail shapes.

Tools mentioned in this guide

  • Canva: Header images, social graphics, Notes images and simple brand kits. Free; Pro US$144/yr for one person (before tax).
  • Kittl: Typography-heavy graphics and AI-assisted illustrations. Free plan; Pro $19/mo or $168/yr ($14/mo billed yearly); Expert $49/mo or $468/yr ($39/mo billed yearly); Max $85/mo or $780/yr ($65/mo billed yearly) (USD, US prices as shown on Kittl's live pricing cards). Prices vary by country; confirm at checkout.
  • Figma: Custom visual systems, templates and detailed design work. Free Starter; Professional full seat $16/mo.
  • Coolors: Picking one consistent color palette for headers, Notes images and your site. Free forever (with ads): palettes up to 5 colors, 10K+ ready color schemes, save up to 10 palettes, 1 project and 1 collection. Pro from $3/mo (per Coolors' own upgrade prompt) adds no ads, up to 10 colors, unlimited saves, palette contrast checks and Coolors AI (3,000 credits/mo).
  • Unsplash: Free, high-quality photos for post headers. Free to use under the Unsplash License (commercial use allowed, no attribution required). Optional paid Unsplash+ subscription for exclusive images under a separate license.
  • Pexels: Free stock photos and short videos for post headers and social previews, as an alternative to Unsplash. Free. Every photo and video on Pexels can be downloaded and used for free under the Pexels License; no paid plan.
  • Squoosh: Shrinking header and in-post images before you upload them. Free. Open-source (Apache 2.0) web app from Google Chrome Labs; no account or paid plan.
  • remove.bg: One-click background removal for headshots and product images. Free preview-size downloads (up to 0.25 megapixels) on remove.bg; full-resolution cutouts cost 1 credit each. Subscriptions from $9/mo for 40 credits ($8.10/mo billed yearly), or pay-as-you-go credits.
  • Excalidraw: Quick hand-drawn-style diagrams and process sketches to drop into posts. Free and open source (MIT license); optional Excalidraw+ at $6/user/mo with a 14-day free trial.
  • Datawrapper: Clean, accurate charts, maps and tables for data-driven posts. Free (unlimited charts, maps and tables, with a "Created with Datawrapper" credit); Pro $21/user/mo excl. VAT (removes the credit); Business $39/user/mo excl. VAT.
